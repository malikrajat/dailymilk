Records

Notebook, spreadsheet or app?

The best record system is the one that is still being used in six months. That is not always the most capable one. Here is an honest comparison, including the cases where a paper register wins.

By Rajat Malik · 17 November 2026 · 9 min read

When paper wins

A notebook is not a legacy tool. It is the right tool in several real situations:

  • The round is small and stable. Under about twenty stops with few rate changes, a well-kept register is faster than any device.
  • There is no reliable power or signal on the route, and no way to charge a device mid-round.
  • The person doing the round will not use a phone. A system that is not used is worse than a simple one that is.
  • You need a physical record for a handover, a signature or a dispute.

If paper wins, win properly: one page per shift, a date at the top, a returns column, and a photograph of each completed page stored off the device.

When a spreadsheet wins

A spreadsheet is the strongest option when you already use a computer for billing and want control over the layout. It is excellent at:

  • Custom reports — a pivot table by customer, route or month takes minutes.
  • Bulk rate changes and historical recalculation, if rates are stored per row.
  • Working alongside an accountant who expects a file.
  • Importing into another tool later, provided the data is tidy.

It fails in three predictable ways:

  1. One column per day. Easy to read, almost impossible to total or import. Use one row per delivery.
  2. Copies that drift. "Final", "final-2" and "final-actual" on three devices. Decide on one canonical file.
  3. Formulas nobody understands. A broken formula can silently produce wrong bills for months. Keep the calculation columns simple and check a few rows by hand each month.

The spreadsheet test

Can you answer "what did this customer take in March, and at what rate?" in under a minute, on a phone, without editing anything? If not, the sheet is a storage format, not a working record.

When an app wins

An app is worth its friction when the round has outgrown memory and the records need to be more than a list. The advantages that matter are unglamorous:

  • The rate is captured per entry, so old bills recalculate correctly.
  • Totals and reports are automatic, including CSV and PDF exports for a customer or an accountant.
  • One canonical copy rather than three drifting files.
  • Offline entry that syncs when the connection returns, on a route with no signal.

The costs are real too:

  • Entry is slower than a tick until the routine is learned; expect a week of clumsiness.
  • It depends on a device that can be dropped, lost or left uncharged.
  • You are trusting someone else's software with your business records, so export and ownership matter more than features.

Backup is the real feature

Whatever you choose, the question to ask is not "what does it do?" but "what happens when this is lost?". A simple discipline is enough:

  • Two copies, two places. The working record and a dated copy somewhere else — a cloud drive, a computer, a photographed page.
  • Export on a schedule. Monthly is enough for a small round; weekly is better if the round is the business.
  • Test the restore once. An export nobody has ever opened is not a backup.
  • Keep the format open. CSV or JSON can be read by anything; a proprietary file may not be readable in five years.

Migrating without losing history

Moving systems is where good records are usually lost. A calm migration has five steps:

  1. Pick a cut-over date. The first day of a month is easiest to explain. Do not migrate mid-period.
  2. Freeze the old system. Do not keep entering new data in both. The old system becomes an archive.
  3. Carry forward the balances. Customer opening balances and outstanding payments are the only things that must move exactly; day-level history can stay in the archive.
  4. Enter a week in parallel. Run both systems for a few days and compare the totals. Differences now are cheap to fix.
  5. Keep the archive readable. Export the old data to an open format, label it with the period it covers, and store it with your backups.

The honest answer

Start with the smallest system that answers the three questions from the record-keeping guide — what was delivered, at what rate, and what is owed. Move up only when the current system visibly fails: bills take too long, a customer dispute cannot be resolved, or the round has outgrown one person's memory. A notebook used every day beats an app abandoned in a week.
