Planning a milk delivery route that saves time

A milk round is a fixed obligation at an unforgiving hour. Most of the time saved on a route does not come from a clever app — it comes from removing backtracking, loading the vehicle in the order of the stops, and keeping a written record of what actually happened.

By Rajat Malik · 17 November 2026 · 10 min read

Start with an honest stop list

A route plan is only as good as its list of stops. Build the list once, properly, before planning anything. For each stop you need five things:

  • Address or landmark — specific enough to find in the dark or in rain.
  • Usual quantity in one unit, and the milk types taken.
  • Time window — shops usually need delivery before opening; households often do not care, as long as it is before breakfast.
  • Access notes — gate code, lift, dog, narrow lane, where the crate is left.
  • Payment arrangement — cash on delivery, monthly account, or prepaid.

The access notes are the ones people skip and the ones that cost the most time. "Second gate, not the main one" removes two minutes of searching every single day.

Cluster before you sequence

Speed on a milk round comes from density: many stops in one small area, with as little crossing between areas as possible. Before ordering individual stops, group them into four to eight clusters by geography — a street, a market, a housing block, a village lane.

Clusters have three benefits over a long ordered list:

  1. They survive changes. If one customer joins or leaves, the cluster still works; a finely tuned sequence has to be rebuilt.
  2. They make a helper possible. A second person can take one cluster without needing to know the whole round.
  3. They make loading obvious: each crate maps to one cluster.

A useful rule of thumb

If two stops are within a short walk of each other, they belong in the same cluster even if the addresses look different. Parking and walking usually dominate a round, not driving between stops.

Sequence the stops

Inside a cluster, order stops by the direction you enter and leave, not by quantity or by customer seniority. A simple, repeatable method:

  1. Pin the cluster's entrance and exit. Which road do you arrive from, and which do you leave by?
  2. Order stops so that the vehicle moves forward. Avoid turning around unless the street is a dead end.
  3. Put time-window stops first within the cluster, then the flexible ones.
  4. Keep the same order every day. Repetition is what makes a round fast; a driver who does not have to think about the next stop finishes sooner.

Do not optimise the order mathematically unless you enjoy it. A sequence that is followed consistently beats an optimal sequence that is re-planned every day and never learned.

Load the vehicle in reverse

The cheapest time saving on a route is loading. Crates should be packed in the reverse order of delivery, so the first stop's crate is the last one in — nearest the door. On a light vehicle this means:

  • First stop nearest the door, last stop deepest in.
  • One crate per cluster, labelled with the cluster name, not with a sequence number.
  • Cash and the collection book at the front, not buried in the load.
  • Empty crates go in a dedicated slot from the first stop, so they do not have to be sorted later.

Loading in reverse also makes shortages visible before departure: if the first crate is short, you find out on the dairy floor rather than at the customer's gate.

Plan for exceptions, not for the ideal day

A route that only works on a dry Tuesday is not a route. Build the exceptions into the plan:

Customers who are away

Agree in advance whether an away customer's delivery is paused or left. Write it on the route sheet as a zero with a reason. Do not let a driver decide each time — that is how milk is left out and charged for.

Holidays and market days

Demand can double or halve around festivals, weekly markets and school holidays. Note them on the route sheet a week ahead so the vehicle is loaded for the actual day.

Bad weather

Rain slows walking, not driving. If a cluster is mostly walk-up deliveries, expect it to take longer and plan the rest of the round around that. Covered crates and a dry place to leave milk matter more than an alternative sequence.

Vehicle problems

Know which cluster you would drop first if the vehicle fails. Having an answer — and a phone number for each cluster's customers — turns a breakdown into a delay instead of a day of complaints.

Measure the change

Route changes are usually judged by feeling. A written measurement is better and takes almost no time. Record four numbers for a week:

Weekly route log. Record it at the end of the round, not from memory later.
Measure How to record What it tells you
Start and finish time Clock time on the sheet Total round duration, including delays
Stops completed Count of non-zero deliveries Time per stop, and whether the round is growing
Litres carried Load total minus returns Load efficiency and forecasting error
Delays One line: where and why The recurring cause, which is usually one stop

Compare a week before the change with a week after. A difference under about ten minutes a day is noise; a consistent fifteen to twenty minutes is a real improvement worth keeping.

The route sheet

Keep the route sheet and the delivery record as one document. Two documents drift apart, and the one that is not used for billing becomes the one nobody fills in.

A route sheet ordered by cluster. The sequence column is the order the driver actually follows.

Keep the sequence stable for at least two weeks

Changing the order every few days prevents anyone from learning it, and it makes the before-and-after comparison meaningless. Pick a sequence, run it for two weeks, then judge it with the numbers.

A route is a habit, not a puzzle

The goal is not the mathematically shortest path. It is a round that the same person can run in the dark, in the rain, without thinking hard, and that produces a record you can bill from. Cluster, sequence, load in reverse, and write down what happened — that is the whole method.
