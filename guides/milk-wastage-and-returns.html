Reducing milk wastage, returns and shortages

Milk is a perishable product sold on thin margins, so a small daily leak is a large yearly number. The good news is that most wastage on a delivery round is not random. It comes from a short list of causes, and two of them can be fixed with a notebook.

By Rajat Malik · 17 November 2026 · 11 min read

Where the milk actually goes

"Wastage" is used as one word for four different losses. Separating them is the first useful step, because each has a different fix.

Four kinds of loss, and the usual cause of each.
Kind of loss What it looks like Usual cause
Over-ordering Milk returns unsold at the end of the round Ordering yesterday's quantity without adjusting for today
Spoilage Milk is sour or off before it is delivered Cold chain break, old stock used first-in-last-out
Breakage and spillage Cans or pouches damaged in transit Overloading, poor crates, rushed handling
Unrecorded Milk received does not match milk delivered plus returns Missing entries, staff use, measurement drift

Only the first is really a forecasting problem. The others are process problems, and no amount of demand prediction will fix a warm storage room or a crate that splits.

Measure it before you fix it

You do not need software to measure wastage. You need one number per day, recorded at the same point in the round.

Daily variance = milk received + opening stock − milk delivered − recorded returns − closing stock

In a small business the stock terms are often zero, and the formula collapses to received minus delivered minus returns. That is still useful: a gap that repeats at the same size every day is a habit, while a gap that spikes is an event.

One week of variance. The repeating 2-litre gap is the signal.
Day Received Delivered Returns Variance
Mon 220 L 216 L 2 L +2 L
Tue 220 L 217 L 1 L +2 L
Wed 220 L 215 L 3 L +2 L
Thu 220 L 212 L 4 L +4 L
Fri 220 L 217 L 1 L +2 L
Sat 240 L 231 L 7 L +2 L

Two litres a day at ₹58 is about ₹116 a day, or roughly ₹3,500 a month. Thursday's extra two litres is a different question — something happened that day. Record a one-line note for spike days; the note is usually enough to identify the cause without further investigation.

A forecasting rule that works

Most small rounds do not need a forecasting model. They need a rule that is better than "same as yesterday". A short rolling average with a weekday adjustment is enough to remove most over-ordering.

Step 1: use a rolling average, not one day

Take the same weekday for the last four weeks and average it. Four weeks smooths out one-off events without hiding a genuine trend.

Step 2: apply the weekday pattern

Demand is not flat. If Saturdays are consistently 15% higher, order for Saturday rather than for an average day. Build the pattern from your own records, not from a general assumption.

Step 3: add a small buffer, then cap it

Add a buffer for uncertainty, but write the buffer down as a number. "Ten litres of buffer" can be reviewed; "a bit extra" cannot. If the buffer is returned more often than it is sold, it is too large.

Worked example

The last four Saturdays delivered 231, 238, 229 and 236 litres — an average of 233.5. Saturday demand is consistently the week's peak, so order 233 litres plus a 10-litre buffer: 243. If returns on Saturday average more than the buffer for three weeks running, reduce the order rather than the buffer.

Step 4: separate known events from the pattern

A festival, a school holiday or a customer closing for renovation is not noise; it is a known change. Adjust the order for the event and note it, then return to the pattern the next day. Do not rebuild the whole average around one unusual week.

A returns policy customers understand

Ambiguity about returns is expensive. A short written policy, given to each customer when they open an account, prevents most disputes and makes the record unambiguous.

  • When milk can be returned. For example: at the time of delivery if it is visibly damaged or sour, otherwise by a stated time the same morning.
  • How it is credited. At the rate of the delivery it reverses, on the next bill, as a separate line.
  • What is not accepted. Part-used containers, or milk returned after it has been left out — be specific and fair.
  • Pauses. How much notice is needed to stop delivery for a holiday, and whether the crate is collected.

Publish the policy on the bill or a card, not only in conversation. A policy nobody can find is the same as no policy.

Handling, cold chain and breakage

Spoilage and breakage are physical problems with physical fixes:

  • Keep milk cold until it leaves. A cold chain is a chain: one warm hour undoes the rest. Check the storage temperature at the same time each day and write it down.
  • First in, first out. Older stock leaves first. A marker on the crate is enough if everyone uses it.
  • Do not overfill crates. The extra can that splits a crate costs more than the trip it saves.
  • Give every crate a home. A named slot in the vehicle for full and empty crates prevents the end-of-round scramble that breaks things.
  • Clean and dry. Residue and moisture shorten the life of crates and cans, and they are a hygiene risk as well as a cost.

The weekly ten-minute review

Once a week, look at three numbers and write one sentence about each:

  1. Variance. Was the gap the same size every day, or did it spike? If it spiked, what happened?
  2. Returns. Which customers returned milk, and why? A single customer returning repeatedly is a different problem from many customers returning once.
  3. Buffer. Was the extra milk sold or returned? Adjust the number, not the habit.

Ten minutes a week is enough to catch a leak while it is still small. The alternative — discovering at year end that a few litres a day disappeared — is much more expensive.
