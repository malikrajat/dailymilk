Billing

How to calculate milk rates and customer bills

A milk bill is simple arithmetic wrapped in a few decisions: which rate applied on which day, how returns are credited, and what happens to money paid in advance. Get those decisions right once and the arithmetic takes care of itself.

By Rajat Malik · 17 November 2026 · 11 min read

Two pricing models

Most milk delivery businesses use one of two rate structures, and some use both for different customers.

Per-litre pricing

One price per litre, perhaps varied by milk type (cow, buffalo, toned, full cream). The bill is quantity × rate. It is easy to explain, easy to check and the right choice when the milk is consistent.

Composition-based pricing

The price depends on measured fat and SNF. Dairies and larger suppliers use it because the value of milk tracks its composition. It is more accurate and much harder to administer: every delivery needs a measurement, and the customer needs to trust the measurement method.

Pick a billing period and stick to it

A calendar month is the easiest period to explain. A 30-day cycle starting on the 1st or 16th is also common. Whichever you choose, write the period on the bill: "1–30 November 2026" removes almost every dispute about which days were counted.

Per-litre billing, step by step

The calculation is four steps, in this order:

  1. Total the litres delivered in the billing period, excluding returns. If the rate changed during the period, total each rate period separately.
  2. Multiply by the rate in force on each delivery date. Do not multiply the whole month by the latest rate.
  3. Value the returns at the rate they were delivered at and subtract them.
  4. Subtract payments received during the period, and add any opening balance carried from last month.

The formula, written out:

Amount due = opening balance + Σ (litres × rate for that day) − value of returns − payments received

A worked monthly bill

Sharma Stores takes 10 litres every morning. The rate is ₹58 per litre for 1–15 November and ₹60 from 16 November. Two litres were returned on 20 November, and the customer paid ₹10,000 on 25 November. There is no opening balance.

November bill for Sharma Stores — each rate period totalled separately.
Line Days Litres/day Litres Rate Amount
1–15 November 15 10 150 ₹58.00 ₹8,700.00
16–30 November 15 10 150 ₹60.00 ₹9,000.00
Delivered 30 — 300 — ₹17,700.00
Return, 20 November — −2 −2 ₹60.00 −₹120.00
Payment, 25 November — — — — −₹10,000.00
Balance due — — — — ₹7,580.00

Check the arithmetic in the same order as the bill: 8,700 + 9,000 = 17,700; minus 120 = 17,580; minus 10,000 = 7,580. If the customer queries the bill, the two rate lines make the reason obvious without re-reading a month of entries.

Fat and SNF based rates

Composition pricing multiplies quantity by a value derived from the measured fat and SNF percentages. A common shape is:

Rate per litre = (fat % × fat rate) + (SNF % × SNF rate)

The two multipliers come from your dairy's rate card, not from a national formula, so they change over time and between buyers. A worked illustration with a fat rate of ₹6.00 per fat point and an SNF rate of ₹2.50 per SNF point:

Illustrative composition rate for one delivery of 200 litres.
Measure Value Multiplier Component
Fat 4.0% ₹6.00 ₹24.00
SNF 8.5% ₹2.50 ₹21.25
Rate per litre — — ₹45.25
200 litres — — ₹9,050.00

Record the measurement, not just the price

Once a composition-based rate is calculated, the price alone cannot be audited. Store the fat reading, the SNF reading and the multipliers alongside the delivery. Without them, a disputed bill cannot be rechecked.

Rate changes, returns and advances

Mid-period rate changes

Split the period at the change date and total each part separately, as in the worked bill above. The alternative — applying the new rate to the whole month — overcharges or undercharges every customer, and the error is invisible unless someone recalculates the month by hand.

Returns and shortages

A return is milk that was delivered and came back; a shortage is milk that never arrived. They are different events and should not be netted into the delivery line. Value returns at the rate of the delivery they reverse, and record the reason — sour, damaged, customer away, over- delivered. The reason is what tells you whether the problem is quality, forecasting or the route.

Advances and partial payments

A customer who pays ₹5,000 in advance has a credit, not a payment against a bill that does not exist yet. Keep advances on their own line and apply them to the next period's bill. When a customer pays part of a bill, record the payment with its date and leave the remainder as the balance; never edit the original amount.

Rounding

Round only the final amount, and state the convention on the bill ("amounts rounded to the nearest rupee"). Rounding each line and then adding produces totals that do not match a customer's own arithmetic.

Reconciling the supplier's bill

The same discipline applies in reverse. Your supplier's bill should reconcile against your intake records:

  • Litres received per day, per shift, against the delivery challans or your own intake sheet.
  • Rate actually charged, compared with the rate agreed for that date.
  • Returns or rejections credited at the right rate.
  • Opening balance carried from last month, and payments made during this one.

A recurring difference of a few litres a day is usually a recording habit rather than fraud: a can measured once and assumed thereafter, or a split delivery written on one line. Track it for a week before concluding anything.

Month-end checklist

  1. Fix the billing period and write it on every bill.
  2. Total deliveries per rate period, not per month.
  3. Value returns at the delivery rate and record the reason.
  4. Carry the opening balance forward unchanged.
  5. Apply advances and partial payments as dated credits.
  6. Round the final amount only, and say so on the bill.
  7. Reconcile delivered litres against milk received and returns; explain any gap before closing the month.
  8. Export or photograph the month's records before starting the next one.
