Records

How to keep daily milk delivery records

A round generates dozens of small facts every day: who took milk, how much, at what rate, and whether it was paid for. Written down, those facts answer every question a milk business asks. Left in memory, they turn into arguments.

By Rajat Malik · 17 November 2026 · 12 min read

Why paper records fail first

Most milk rounds start with a notebook, and that is not a mistake. A notebook is fast, cheap and never runs out of battery. It fails for a different reason: the same notebook has to serve three jobs at once — a delivery checklist, a price list and a debt ledger — and those jobs need different views of the same data.

The delivery checklist is read at 5 a.m. in the dark. The price list is read once a month when rates change. The ledger is read when a customer says "I already paid for those six days". One page cannot be optimised for all three, so the ledger gets written last, in a hurry, and becomes the part nobody trusts.

The fix is not necessarily software. It is deciding, in advance, what a complete entry looks like and writing every entry that way — whether it goes on paper, in a spreadsheet or into an app.

The seven fields every entry needs

A milk entry is one line: a quantity delivered to one customer, or received from one supplier, on one date and shift. Seven fields make it complete. If a field is missing, the line can still be read, but it cannot be reconciled later.

The minimum data set for a milk delivery entry.
Field Why it is needed Example
Date Groups entries into days, weeks and billing periods. 2026-11-17
Shift Morning and evening rounds are separate deliveries; merging them hides mistakes. Morning
Milkman / route Shows who delivered, and lets you compare routes. Route B — Suresh
Customer or supplier The party the quantity is owed to or owed by. Sharma Stores
Quantity In one consistent unit. Litres are usual; record half-litres as 0.5, never as "half". 12.5 L
Rate The price in force on that date, not today's price. Without it, a mid-month change rewrites history. ₹58 / L
Status or note Paid, due, returned, cancelled or adjusted — and why, in a few words. 2 L returned, sour

Two optional fields are worth adding if you sell on composition. The first is fat and SNF (solid-not-fat), because composition-based rates cannot be recalculated without them. The second is a payment date, kept separately from the delivery date: a customer can owe for ten days and settle all of it at once, and the ledger should show both facts.

One unit, one decimal convention

Pick litres and stay with litres. Write 0.5, 1.5 and 12.25 rather than "half", "one and a half" or "12¼". Mixed units are the single most common reason two totals disagree at month end.

The five-minute daily routine

Record keeping fails when it is treated as an evening task. By evening, the details have blurred and the entries become guesses. Five minutes at the right moments is enough.

Before the round: one minute

Write the date and shift at the top of the sheet before the first stop. That sounds trivial, but it prevents the most common paper error: two days of entries on one page with no boundary between them.

At each stop: ten seconds

Write the quantity as it goes into the crate, not from memory at the end. If a customer is away, write 0 — away rather than leaving a gap. A gap is ambiguous; a zero is information.

After the round: two minutes

Do three checks before you put the sheet away:

  • Count the lines. They should match the number of stops on the route, including the zeroes.
  • Total the litres. Compare the total with what left the dairy. Any difference is a leak you can still explain today.
  • Write the return quantity. Unsold or returned milk is a separate line, never a negative quantity hidden inside a delivery.

Once a week: five minutes

Add up the week's litres per customer and compare it with the previous week. You are not looking for exact equality — demand moves — but for a customer whose usage has halved without explanation, or a route whose total has drifted up while the customer count has not.

The 30-minute month-end close

A month-end close is not bookkeeping in the accounting sense. It is four reconciliations that let you send bills with confidence.

  1. Lock the period. Choose a cut-off — the last delivery of the month — and do not add late entries to it. If a correction is needed, enter it as an adjustment in the next period with a note.
  2. Total litres per customer. Multiply each day's quantity by the rate that applied on that day, then sum. If your records only store today's rate, this is where they break.
  3. Subtract payments and returns. Payments recorded during the period reduce the balance. Returns reduce the quantity owed. Keep them on separate lines so the bill can show both.
  4. Reconcile against supply. Milk received from your supplier minus milk delivered to customers minus recorded returns should be near zero. A persistent gap of a few litres a day is usually unrecorded staff milk, breakage or a measurement habit.

When the four totals agree, send the bills. When they do not, fix the records before sending anything: a corrected bill is awkward, but a wrong bill is worse.

The three mistakes that cost the most

1. Storing only the current rate

Rates change. If an entry does not carry the rate that applied when it was delivered, recalculating an old bill silently uses the new price. The fix is to snapshot the rate on every entry, even when it feels repetitive.

2. Mixing returns into deliveries

Writing "10 L delivered, 2 L back" as "8 L delivered" makes the customer's usage look lower than it is, and hides the fact that quality or over-ordering is a recurring problem. Record the delivery and the return separately.

3. Treating the notebook as the only copy

A notebook left on a scooter seat in the rain is a business risk. If paper is your primary record, photograph each completed sheet and store the images somewhere that is not the same phone. If you use a spreadsheet, keep a dated copy off the device. If you use an app, use its export.

A free daily sheet to copy

This is the layout used in the worked examples throughout these guides. It fits a single page for a round of about 40 stops.

Daily delivery sheet — one row per stop, one sheet per shift.
# Customer Qty (L) Rate Amount Return (L) Paid Note
1 Sharma Stores 12.5 58.00 725.00 0 No —
2 Green Cafe 8.0 58.00 464.00 1.0 Yes Returned sour
3 R. Verma 2.0 58.00 116.00 0 No Away until 20th
… … … … … … … …

The column order matters more than it looks: quantity, rate and amount sit together so the arithmetic can be checked at a glance, and returns and payment status sit at the end so they are never skipped when the round is running late.

If you use a spreadsheet

Keep one row per entry, never one column per day. A sheet with dates across the top is easy to read and very hard to total, sort or import later. One row per delivery is the format every reporting tool expects.

What "good enough" looks like

Good records are not perfect records. They are records that let you answer three questions without guessing: what did this customer take last month, what did it cost at the time, and what do they still owe? If your system answers those three, it is working — even if it is a notebook.
